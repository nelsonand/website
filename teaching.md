    <h2>How I teach</h2>
    <div class="cols">
      <div><h3>Fundamentals before complexity</h3><p>Students need a rigorous grasp of the underlying physics and mathematics before they can reason reliably about complex systems.</p></div>
      <div><h3>Learning through authentic problems</h3><p>Engineering judgment develops when students make real decisions under realistic constraints and have to defend the trade-offs they choose.</p></div>
      <div><h3>Structure first, then independence</h3><p>Students learn best when expectations are explicit and feedback is frequent, and when responsibility shifts to them as their skills grow.</p></div>
    </div>
    <p>I approach teaching the way I approach research: as an iterative, evidence-driven process. I set clear objectives, try strategies grounded in educational research, look at what students actually learned, and revise.</p>

<div class="section" id="experience">
  <div class="label"><div class="num">02</div><div class="t">Experience</div></div>
  <div class="body">
    <h2>What my teaching has taught me</h2>
    <h3>Structured activity builds reasoning</h3>
    <p>As an Assistant in Instruction for an undergraduate course at Princeton, I advocated for turning chalkboard-only lectures into a <strong>studio-style "flipped" classroom</strong>. Class time became guided group work that started with structured problem sets and built toward open-ended challenges, such as preparing and defending the physics basis for a congressional geoengineering proposal. I went on to complete Princeton's McGraw Center Teaching Transcript program, where I also learned that technical communication has to be explicitly taught, practiced, and assessed.</p>
    <h3>Scaffolded design projects build systems-level judgment</h3>
    <p>I helped organize and teach <strong>two multi-university courses on fusion power plant design</strong>, held jointly between MIT, Princeton, and Columbia. Student teams learned to integrate plasma physics, magnets, thermal management, materials, and cost into one self-consistent design, and to confront the fact that improving one subsystem often degrades another. With staged milestones and regular expert feedback, the students produced professional-quality work: the first offering yielded <a href="{{ '/2023-10-29-nt-reactors/' | relative_url }}">three peer-reviewed papers authored by students</a>, and the teams produced the MANTA <a href="https://doi.org/10.1088/1361-6587/ad6708">[PPCF 2024]</a> and CENTAUR <a href="https://doi.org/10.1088/1361-6587/ae9eea">[PPCF 2026]</a> pilot plant concepts.</p>
    <h3>Student ownership sustains learning communities</h3>
    <p>At Columbia, I founded a <strong>student-led graduate plasma seminar course</strong> to supplement limited course offerings, then deliberately stepped back so graduate students could run it themselves. Years later it remains a staple of the Columbia plasma curriculum, organized entirely by students.</p>

<div class="section" id="courses">
  <div class="label"><div class="num">03</div><div class="t">Courses</div></div>
  <div class="body">
    <h2>Courses I would like to teach</h2>
    <p>I am eager to teach core undergraduate courses across the thermal-fluid sciences, computation, and design sequences of a mechanical engineering curriculum. I would also like to develop two electives built around my expertise:</p>
    <div class="aims two">
      <div class="aim">
        <div class="n">Senior / graduate elective</div>
        <h3>Fusion Energy Engineering</h3>
        <p>A project-based course in which teams carry a fusion power plant from requirements to integrated conceptual design, combining thermodynamics, heat transfer, structural and electromagnetic design, materials, energy conversion, and cost. It is fundamentally a course in <strong>systems engineering and design trade-offs</strong>, with fusion as an exceptionally demanding test case, and it requires no prior plasma coursework.</p>
        <p>Teams use open-source design tools such as FUSE and TokaMaker, and the semester ends with a formal design review before a panel of scientists and engineers.</p>
        <div class="meta">Draft modules: requirements &amp; missions · plasma performance basics · magnets &amp; structures · first wall, divertor &amp; heat removal · blankets &amp; power conversion · cost &amp; availability · design review</div>
      </div>
      <div class="aim">
        <div class="n">Graduate elective</div>
        <h3>Data-Driven Design of Engineering Systems</h3>
        <p>Engineers increasingly make decisions when simulations are expensive, data are limited, and system behavior is uncertain. This course teaches surrogate modeling, uncertainty quantification, and Bayesian and multi-objective optimization as tools for making those decisions, using datasets drawn from thermal-fluid, energy, and fusion systems.</p>
        <p>It is designed to serve students across mechanical engineering, from thermal-fluid sciences to computational design.</p>
        <div class="meta">Draft modules: design of experiments · Gaussian processes &amp; surrogates · uncertainty propagation · Bayesian calibration · multi-objective &amp; robust optimization · value of information</div>
      </div>
    </div>
    <h3>Methods matched to goals</h3>
    <p>Short in-class problem-solving and peer instruction develop analytical reasoning. Computational notebooks, where students change a parameter and watch a design respond, develop modeling intuition. Team design projects develop systems thinking, and oral design reviews develop technical communication and professional judgment. Frequent low-stakes assessments let both me and my students catch misconceptions early.</p>
    <p>Following the model of my Columbia seminar, I also hope to help launch a <strong>student-led graduate seminar in fusion science and engineering</strong> that is open to students across engineering and physics.</p>
  </div>

<div class="section" id="inclusive">
  <div class="label"><div class="num">04</div><div class="t">Every student</div></div>
  <div class="body">
    <h2>Teaching every student in the room</h2>
    <p>Leading the APS-DPP CONNECT committee and APS-DPP Student Day taught me that small structural choices help determine who feels they belong in engineering.</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>In the classroom:</strong> explicit learning objectives and rubrics for every assignment; multiple ways to demonstrate mastery (written analyses, code, and oral design reviews); coursework built on free, open-source software so that no student is limited by access to licenses or computing; and anonymous mid-semester feedback, with a report back to students on what I am changing in response.</li>
      <li><strong>In my research group:</strong> written mentoring and individual development plans revisited each semester; structured onboarding with shared code, documentation, and paired projects; and paid undergraduate research positions for students who cannot afford to volunteer their time.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

  <div class="body">
    <h2>Interactive tools for learning</h2>
    <p>As an undergraduate at CU Boulder, I benefited directly from research-based teaching, including the PhET interactive simulations. Interactive resources that build physical intuition for fusion systems remain scarce. Building on my work with Princeton's <a href="https://www.pppl.gov/remote-glow-discharge-experiment-rgdx">Remote Glow Discharge Experiment</a> and with Next Step Fusion on <a href="https://fusiontwin.io/">web-based fusion simulation tools</a>, I am the PI on a proposal under review with the NSF Improving Undergraduate STEM Education program to develop interactive simulations for plasma physics education.</p>
